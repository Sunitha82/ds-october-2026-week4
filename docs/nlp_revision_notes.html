1. Tokenization — split text into pieces

Tokenization means splitting text into smaller pieces called tokens. A token may be a whole word, part of a word or punctuation. Think of cutting a sentence into individual cards so a model can work with the pieces.

“The curry tastes delicious!”
↓ Split into tokens
Thecurrytastesdelicious!
Five tokens in this example

Why do we tokenize?

Tokenization identifies the pieces of text to represent and process. Those pieces are then mapped to numerical representations that a machine learning model can use.

OperationWhat it does
TokenizationSplits text into pieces.
Numerical representationRepresents those pieces using numbers.
Sentiment classificationA trained model uses numerical inputs to predict sentiment.

Subword tokenization

A tokenizer may split a word into smaller parts. For illustration, unhelpful → [un] [help] [ful]. This is still tokenization. Using smaller pieces can help models handle unfamiliar words through familiar parts.

Important: Exact splits depend on the tokenizer. One token does not always equal one word. Tokenization alone does not decide meaning or sentiment.
Interview answer: “Tokenization splits text into smaller units, such as words or subwords, before they are converted into numerical representations.”

Questions from our lesson

“The delivery was very slow.” Has tokenization decided this is negative?

No. It has only split the text: [The] [delivery] [was] [very] [slow] [.]. Your answer correctly identified this distinction.

Is [un] [help] [ful] still tokenization?

Yes. This is subword tokenization. Tokens do not have to be whole words. You answered this correctly.

2. Stemming — trim related words

Stemming shortens words by cutting off endings using rules. Think of trimming branches back to a common stem.

Original wordPossible stem
connectedconnect

Practical example: searching support tickets

You search for “connect.” Stemming can help match tickets containing “connected,” “connecting” and “connections,” by representing these forms using a common stem.

studies   →   studi
studying   →   studi

Different word forms → a shared stem

“Studi” is not a dictionary word. This does not necessarily mean the stemmer failed: its goal is to group related forms. Exact outputs vary with the stemming algorithm.

Interview answer: “Stemming removes word endings using rules to group related words. The result may not be a valid dictionary word.”
A stemmer changes “studying” to “studi.” Has it necessarily failed?

No. It can still group “studies” and “studying” together. Your answer was: it groups related forms together.

3. Lemmatization — find the dictionary form

Lemmatization converts a word into its dictionary base form, called its lemma. Think of finding the word’s main dictionary entry.

WordBase form
studyingstudy
studiesstudy
wentgo
micemouse

Lemmatization uses vocabulary and grammatical information. “Went” becoming “go” cannot be achieved by simply trimming the end of the word.

Why grammatical information matters

“I saw a bird.”Verb: past tense of “see”see
“This saw cuts wood.”Noun: a cutting toolsaw

The appropriate grammatical information helps the lemmatizer choose the right form. Some tools need a part-of-speech tag supplied; they do not all infer the full context automatically.

Memory aid: “Stemming trims the word; lemmatization finds its dictionary base form.”
A search system converts “went” into “go.” Which technique is this, and why?

Lemmatization. It identifies the dictionary form rather than just cutting off letters. You correctly identified lemmatization and added that it checks grammatical information.

4. Stop word removal — keep what matters to the task

Stop word removal removes common words when they add little useful information for a particular task. Examples often include “the,” “a,” “is” and “and.”

“The farmer grows rice and wheat.”
↓ Remove selected common words
“farmer grows rice wheat”

For a system identifying article subjects, the main topic may remain clear after this removal. Removing selected common words can reduce the text a traditional NLP system processes.

A common word can still be essential

OriginalIf “not” is removedWhat goes wrong?
The food is not good.food goodThe meaning is reversed.
The service was not helpful.service helpfulA negative review appears positive.
Rule: Common does not mean useless. Preserve words that change meaning. Choose cleaning based on the task and model; do not apply every preprocessing step automatically.

Transformer models usually retain these words because they contribute to context and grammar. Removing punctuation, special characters or emojis is a separate cleaning decision, not stop word removal.

Interview answer: “Stop word removal removes common words that add little value to a task, but we must preserve words that affect meaning.”
Would you remove “not” from “The service was not helpful” in sentiment analysis?

No. “Not” changes the sentiment of “helpful.” Your answer correctly recognised that it affects positive or negative meaning. More precisely, its effect comes from its relationship with the surrounding words.

5. Bag of Words — count words

Bag of Words represents text using word counts. Imagine putting the words in a bag: you can count them, but their original order is lost.

Step 1: build a vocabulary

For the reviews “good food” and “good good service,” our vocabulary is [food, good, service]. A vocabulary is the list of distinct words being represented.

Step 2: count words in the fixed vocabulary order

ReviewfoodgoodserviceVector
good food110[1, 1, 0]
good good service021[0, 2, 1]
good service service012[0, 1, 2]

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. Each position must consistently refer to the same vocabulary word. A zero means that word is absent. These count vectors can be inputs to a model, such as a spam classifier.

Limitation 1: word order disappears

“Dog bites man” and “Man bites dog” have identical individual-word counts, although their meanings differ.

ReviewgoodbadnotMeaning
The food is good, not bad.111Positive
The food is bad, not good.111Negative

The other words also have matching counts. Basic Bag of Words does not preserve which word “not” applies to.

Clarification from our discussion: Having both “good” and “bad” does not mean Bag of Words has decided “neutral.” Bag of Words produces a representation, not a sentiment decision. A classifier using only identical count vectors cannot distinguish these two reviews.

Limitation 2: many zeros — sparse vectors

With a vocabulary of 10,000 words, a short review contains very few of those words. Most vector entries are therefore zero. A representation containing mostly zeros is called sparse.

Interview answer: “Bag of Words converts text into word-count vectors, but ignores word order and produces sparse representations.”
What is the vector for “good service service” with vocabulary [food, good, service]?

[0, 1, 2]. You answered correctly: no “food,” one “good,” two “service.”

Why do the positive and negative reviews above have the same vector?

They contain the same words with the same counts. The problem is losing word order and relationships, not incorrect counting. You correctly identified the loss of order.

6. TF-IDF — weight distinctive words

TF-IDF weights a word using its frequency in one document and its rarity across a collection of documents. In our examples, one ticket is one document; all tickets together form the document collection.

TicketText
AApplication error database database
BApplication error login
CApplication error payment

“Application” and “error” appear everywhere, so they do little to distinguish the tickets. “Database” appears twice in A and nowhere else, making it a useful clue about A’s subject.

TF: Term Frequency — look inside one document

TF measures how frequently a word appears within the document. In a simple raw-count version, four mentions give TF = 4 and one mention gives TF = 1. Some versions adjust for document length or use logarithmic counts.

IDF: Inverse Document Frequency — look across documents

IDF measures how uncommon a word is across the document collection. A word present in fewer documents gets higher IDF. This uses how many documents contain the word, not how often the word repeats inside one document.

TF-IDF = TF × IDF
Memory aid: “Common here, rare elsewhere.”
Frequent in this document + uncommon across documents → a higher weight, all else equal.
PartQuestionWhat increases it?
TFHow often does it appear in this ticket?More occurrences within the ticket, using raw-count TF.
IDFHow many tickets contain it?Fewer tickets containing the word.

Worked comparison 1: same word, different tickets

Two tickets have the same length. “Database” appears once in A and four times in B. Its IDF is the same for both because they use the same document collection.

TicketRaw TFIDFUnnormalised TF-IDF
A1Same IDF value1 × IDF
B4Same IDF value4 × IDF

B has the higher weight in this simple calculation. The “rare means higher” rule belongs to IDF across documents; it does not mean fewer repetitions inside one ticket give higher TF.

Precision: These comparisons use the simple TF × IDF calculation before normalising the full document vector. Real implementations may smooth IDF, transform TF or normalise vectors; exact final numbers depend on those choices. Equal ticket lengths alone do not guarantee the same normalisation factor.

Worked comparison 2: same TF, different rarity

In a collection of 100 tickets:

WordOccurrences in this ticketTickets containing itResult
application4100Lower IDF
timeout45Higher IDF and higher TF-IDF in this ticket

“Timeout” receives the higher weight. Both words have the same TF, but “timeout” is rarer across the collection.

What TF-IDF does—and does not—capture

Think of highlighting words that distinguish a document. The weights are calculated from word frequencies, not from an understanding of meaning. Basic individual-word TF-IDF still loses word order and usually produces sparse vectors. It does not solve the “good, not bad” versus “bad, not good” example.

Interview answer: “TF-IDF gives more weight to words that occur frequently in a document but are uncommon across the document collection.”

Pending check: Does basic individual-word TF-IDF understand “not good” as a phrase?

No. It assigns weights to the individual words. It does not preserve the relationship between “not” and “good.” A downstream classifier may learn associations from those features, but the representation itself does not understand the phrase.

7. Maths refresher: dot product

Multiply matching positions in two vectors, then add the products. The two vectors must contain the same number of components.

A = [2, 3, 4]   B = [5, 1, 2]
PositionABProduct
First252 × 5 = 10
Second313 × 1 = 3
Third424 × 2 = 8
A · B = 10 + 3 + 8 = 21

The result is one number, called a scalar, rather than another vector.

Memory aid: “Multiply matching positions, then add.”

In transformer attention, a dot product helps calculate how strongly a query matches a key. We have not yet studied those roles in detail; they will be covered step by step later.

Earlier practice: A = [1, 2], B = [3, 4]. What is A · B?

(1 × 3) + (2 × 4) = 3 + 8 = 11. This question was previously left unanswered.

How the text-processing ideas fit together

Text can be tokenized, optionally normalised or cleaned where appropriate, represented numerically using a method such as Bag of Words or TF-IDF, and then supplied to a model for a task such as sentiment classification. This is a conceptual flow, not a mandatory recipe: the exact preprocessing depends on the task and model.

Keep the roles separate: Splitting words, cleaning words and creating numerical features are different from training a model to make a prediction.

9. Follow-up scenario questions

Answer these in your own words. Each answer includes the reasoning you could explain to a trainer.

1. A sentence becomes six tokens. Does that mean it contained six words?

No. Tokens can be whole words, subwords or punctuation. The count depends on the tokenizer.

2. A ticket search system groups “connecting” and “connected” under “connect.” Why might this help?

It can retrieve related word forms without requiring an exact match to the original spelling. This illustrates the purpose of stemming.

3. You need “mice” converted to “mouse.” Which technique is appropriate?

Lemmatization: it can identify irregular dictionary forms, rather than just trim an ending.

4. A sentiment system changes “I am not happy” into “happy.” What went wrong?

Cleaning removed meaningful negation and reversed the message. Stop word removal must be chosen for the task.

5. With vocabulary [error, login, payment], represent “login error error.”

[2, 1, 0]. Preserve the vocabulary order: two errors, one login, no payment.

6. Your vocabulary contains 20,000 words, but a ticket uses only 12 distinct vocabulary words. Why is its Bag of Words vector sparse?

Only 12 positions have nonzero counts; the other 19,988 are zero. Sparse means mostly zero entries.

7. Would individual-word Bag of Words distinguish “server calls client” from “client calls server”?

No. Both have the same word counts, although the direction of the action changes.

8. “Error” occurs in 900 of 1,000 tickets; “deadlock” occurs in 10. Which receives higher IDF?

Deadlock. It occurs in fewer documents. IDF concerns rarity across documents.

9. Deadlock appears five times in one ticket. Does it count as appearing in five documents for IDF?

No. It contributes five occurrences to raw TF in that ticket, but only one containing document to its document frequency.

10. A word appears very frequently everywhere. Is frequency alone enough to give it high TF-IDF?

No. TF and IDF combine. Widespread occurrence lowers IDF, reducing its usefulness for distinguishing documents.

11. Does a higher TF-IDF weight mean a word is more positive?

No. It indicates frequency-based distinctiveness, not positive sentiment.

12. Does replacing word counts with TF-IDF automatically fix word-order problems?

No. Basic individual-word TF-IDF changes the weights but still does not preserve word order.

13. Calculate [2, 1, 3] · [4, 0, 2].

(2 × 4) + (1 × 0) + (3 × 2) = 8 + 0 + 6 = 14.

14. Explain TF and IDF to your trainer in two sentences.

“TF measures how often a word appears within a document. IDF gives more weight to words that occur in fewer documents across the collection.”
