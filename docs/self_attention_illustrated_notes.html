Self-Attention — Complete Study Notes

Source: The supplied Jay Patel transcript, from the introduction to the unfinished sentence at approximately 42:15.

Scope: All teaching points, examples, calculations, analogies, benefits and recap material in the supplied text are covered below. Repeated explanations are consolidated. The speaker's opening comments about filming and restructuring the lesson are presentation background; his teaching approach is to build self-attention from the problem to the equation. The supplied text ends before he explains division by √dₖ. That explanation below is labelled as an addition.

Main idea: Self-attention creates a representation of each token by combining information from relevant tokens in the same sequence.

The transcript says “words”; real transformer inputs are usually tokens, which can be whole words, parts of words or punctuation. We keep the transcript's word-level examples to make the calculations easy.

1. Why self-attention matters — 0:00–1:14

Self-attention is a central component of transformers, including models such as BERT and GPT. It helps a model interpret an item using the context around it. It is a component of a neural network, rather than an entire model by itself.

The lesson builds the mechanism in this order: identify the context problem; combine word vectors; calculate relationship scores; normalize them with softmax; introduce learnable projections; express the complete operation as matrix multiplication.

2. The problem: the same word can have different meanings — 1:15–4:04

The problem is contextual word representation: the numerical representation should reflect how the word is being used in this particular sentence.

Memory aid: The surrounding words provide clues about the intended meaning.

3. Static word embeddings and their limitation

A vector is an ordered list of numbers. An embedding is a vector used to represent an item such as a word.

With a traditional static embedding lookup, the same word receives the same starting vector in different sentences. For example, the initial vector for “Apple” can be the same in “I love Apple phones” and “I love apple juice”. The vector alone does not identify which sense is intended in this occurrence.

The speaker imagines this initial Apple vector as “50% technology and 50% fruit”. This is an analogy, not a literal description of embedding coordinates. Learned dimensions do not normally have neat labels such as “is fruit”, “can call” or “is smart”.

Correction to the transcript: RNNs and LSTMs can create contextual hidden states even when their input embeddings are static. Their important limitations include sequential computation and difficulty learning some long-distance relationships. It is incorrect to say that all RNN models cannot represent context.

Also, “static across occurrences” does not necessarily mean “frozen throughout training”: an embedding table can be trained while still assigning one lookup vector to each token ID.

4. What self-attention produces — 4:05–6:05

For “I love Apple phones”, self-attention allows the representation at Apple to incorporate information from phones. For “I love apple juice”, it allows information from juice to contribute instead.

Input occurrence Relevant contextual clue Desired interpretation
Apple in “Apple phones” phones Technology company
Apple in “apple juice” juice Fruit
light in “lightweight” Weight-related context Low weight
light in “light blue” blue Colour shade
light in “light-headed” headed / surrounding context Faint or dizzy

The speaker illustrates this as movement in an embedding space: toward technology-related, fruit-related, colour-related or dizziness-related meanings.

Treat this movement as intuition. Learned attention does not guarantee a literal movement toward a named meaning in a visible 2D map. Its output is a new activation; it does not overwrite the stored embedding table during inference.

5. First construction: make a weighted mixture — 6:06–10:14

The transcript shortens its running examples to three words: love, Apple, phones and love, apple, juice.

Let E(word) mean the original embedding and word′ (“word dash” or “word prime”) mean its new representation.

For the technology sentence:

Apple′ = 0.1 E(love) + 0.5 E(Apple) + 0.4 E(phones)

For the fruit sentence:

Apple′ = 0.1 E(love) + 0.5 E(apple) + 0.4 E(juice)

The percentages mean 10% of one vector, 50% of another and 40% of the last. Each vector is multiplied by its weight; the resulting vectors are added coordinate by coordinate. This is a weighted sum. Since these particular weights sum to one, it is also a weighted average.

The transcript also gives:

love′ = 0.8 E(love) + 0.1 E(Apple) + 0.1 E(phones)

juice′ = 0.1 E(love) + 0.4 E(apple) + 0.5 E(juice)

Adding apple information to juice can help its representation describe apple juice specifically.

These weights are invented teaching examples. They are not measured results, fixed rules, or the output of the numerical example later in the lesson.

6. What the mixture weights mean — 10:15–13:30

For the Apple output, the speaker interprets 0.4 as the relationship of Apple to phones, 0.5 as its relationship to itself and 0.1 as its relationship to love.

The operational question is: “When updating this word, how much information should I take from each available word?”

The weights can differ for every output position. Apple may need considerable information from phones, while phones may need a different amount from Apple.

The speaker uses a 2D embedding diagram: related words tend to occur near one another, and Apple is illustrated between phones and juice. This introduces similarity scores.

Precision: Embedding geometry depends on training and the similarity measure. Far-apart words are not necessarily opposites; antonyms may be close because they occur in similar contexts. Attention compatibility also need not mean that words are synonyms.

7. Dot product: calculate one score from two vectors — 13:31–16:23

Rule: multiply matching coordinates, then add the products.

For A = [a₁, a₂] and B = [b₁, b₂]:

A · B = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂

For more dimensions, continue the same multiply-and-add pattern across every coordinate. The result is one number, called a scalar.

The coordinates implied by the transcript's arithmetic are:

Word Illustrative vector
Apple [2, 2]
phones [0, 4]
love [−4, 3]
juice [4, 0]
Pair Calculation Raw score
Apple and phones 2 × 0 + 2 × 4 8
Apple and love 2 × (−4) + 2 × 3 −2
Apple and juice 2 × 4 + 2 × 0 8
Apple and itself 2 × 2 + 2 × 2 8

The phone and juice calculations belong to their respective example sentences. They do not mean both must appear in the same sequence.

Why a transpose appears

Using row vectors, E(Apple) has shape 1 × 2. Transposing E(phones) changes its shape from 1 × 2 to 2 × 1.

(1 × 2) × (2 × 1) = (1 × 1)

Thus E(Apple) E(phones)ᵀ produces the single dot-product score.

Correction: dot product is not simply distance

The transcript suggests that a smaller distance always means a higher dot product. In general this is false: dot products depend on both direction and vector length.

For example, A = [1, 0], B = [1, 0], C = [100, 0]. B is closer to A, but A · C = 100 is larger than A · B = 1.

Use the dot product here as a compatibility score. It is not a distance, a percentage or a probability.

8. Softmax: convert raw scores into attention weights — 16:24–17:28

Raw dot products can be negative, large or small, and do not generally sum to one. To form a normalized mixture, the lesson applies softmax.

softmax(sᵢ) = exp(sᵢ) / Σⱼ exp(sⱼ)

Softmax makes the weights nonnegative and makes their sum one. For finite unmasked scores, the mathematical outputs are strictly positive. Larger scores receive larger weights.

For Apple attending to [love, Apple, phones], the raw scores are:

[−2, 8, 8]

Their softmax is approximately:

[0.0000227, 0.4999887, 0.4999887]

The speaker rounds this to [0.0, 0.5, 0.5]. The first value is nearly zero, rather than exactly zero.

These are attention weights over the available positions. A weight of 0.5 does not mean a 50% probability that Apple is the technology company.

9. Use the weights to create the new vector — 17:29–19:21

In the initial construction:

Apple′ = α₁E(love) + α₂E(Apple) + α₃E(phones)

Here α₁, α₂ and α₃ are the softmax weights. Repeat this process for love and phones to get their new representations too.

Worked continuation, added for clarity: Using the rounded weights above,

Apple′ ≈ 0[−4, 3] + 0.5[2, 2] + 0.5[0, 4] = [1, 3].

For [love, apple, juice], the corresponding rounded mixture is:

Apple′ ≈ 0[−4, 3] + 0.5[2, 2] + 0.5[4, 0] = [3, 1].

The same starting Apple vector now produces different outputs because the other words differ. These calculations still use the simplified method, before learnable Q/K/V projections.

10. The gravity analogy — 19:22–20:09

The speaker compares attention to gravity: objects with a stronger relationship exert a stronger pull, so Apple's representation is pulled toward phones in the technology sentence.

Useful lesson: a word receiving more attention contributes more strongly to the output mixture.

Limits: no physical force is involved, dot products are not gravitational distances, and learned attention can be directional. Unlike physical gravity, Apple attending strongly to phones does not require phones to attend equally strongly to Apple.

11. Problems with the first construction — 20:10–26:53

Problem A: no learnable parameters in the attention rule

The initial method only compares and mixes the given embeddings. If those embeddings are held fixed, the attention rule has no parameters that training can adjust to learn more useful relationships. The embeddings could themselves be trainable, so “the whole model has nothing to learn” would be too broad.

Electronic-store chatbot example: A customer says, “I love Apple Watch.” Apple can be associated with fruit or technology; watch can mean observe/see or a device associated with clocks/time. The speaker uses this to motivate learning the product relationship rather than relying entirely on general embedding similarity.

The example illustrates a possible limitation. It is not a guarantee that static embeddings cannot already place Apple and Watch near one another.

Problem B: the same vector plays three different roles

The initial method uses the same embedding as the requesting vector, the matching vector and the information being mixed.

In particular:

E(Apple) · E(phones) = E(phones) · E(Apple)

The raw dot product is symmetric. But the information needed by Apple from phones need not equal the information needed by phones from Apple.

Important nuance: Symmetric raw scores do not guarantee symmetric final attention weights: each row's softmax can have a different denominator.

The speaker's additional concern: excessive self-matching

The transcript says a word's score with itself tends to dominate. This can be a concern, but it is not a mathematical certainty. The dot product with another vector can exceed the self-dot-product. Learned projections can make other matches useful; they do not guarantee that self-attention to the same position disappears.

Proposed solution

Introduce three separately learned matrix transformations. The speaker first describes these loosely as A, B and C; the actual solution uses matrices, not just three scalar multipliers. Scalar multipliers alone would not break the raw-score symmetry in the proposed way.

12. Query, Key and Value — 26:54–27:36

From an input vector x, create:

q = xW_Q
k = xW_K
v = xW_V

Vector Plain-language role What it does
Query, q What information am I looking for? Is compared with available keys
Key, k What can this position be matched on? Helps determine compatibility
Value, v What information can I contribute? Is mixed into the output

These role descriptions are memory aids; Q, K and V are numerical vectors, not literal questions or labels.

W_Q, W_K and W_V are learned parameters. They are adjusted through backpropagation and an optimizer during training. At inference, the learned matrices are normally fixed while Q/K/V activations and attention weights are calculated for the current input.

Within one head and layer, the same projection matrices are applied at every position. Different input vectors produce different Q/K/V vectors. Different heads or layers can have different matrices.

For the transcript's example:

(1 × 512) × (512 × 64) = (1 × 64)

Each 512-number input produces a 64-number query, key and value. These are illustrative dimensions, not mandatory sizes for all transformers.

13. Complete per-word operation — 27:37–31:21

To calculate the Apple output in [love, Apple, phones]:

  1. Use q_Apple as the query.
  2. Compare it with k_love, k_Apple and k_phones.
  3. Each comparison is a dot product and gives one raw score.
  4. In scaled dot-product attention, divide the scores by √dₖ.
  5. Apply softmax across these scores to get α₁, α₂, α₃.
  6. Calculate z_Apple = α₁v_love + α₂v_Apple + α₃v_phones.

Q and K decide the weights; V supplies the information.

For love's output, use q_love against the same available keys and mix the same available values using love's newly computed weights. For phones, use q_phones.

The raw directional scores can now differ:

q_Apple · k_phones ≠ q_phones · k_Apple, in general.

Separate learned Q and K projections make this possible; they do not require the scores to be unequal in every case.

Three input positions produce three output vectors. Five positions produce five; 100 positions produce 100.

Why “self”? The queries, keys and values are all derived from the same input sequence. It does not mean that each word looks only at itself.

14. Benefits and their limits — 31:22–34:29

Parallel computation

Within a layer, Apple's attention output does not need love's newly calculated output first. All outputs use the layer's existing inputs, so they can be calculated together using matrix operations.

The speaker illustrates this with separate CPU threads and multiple GPUs. Actual implementations use optimized matrix computation on available hardware; they do not require one thread or GPU per word.

An RNN's hidden state at a later position depends on the previous hidden state, which introduces a sequential chain.

Correction: The claim that one word and 1,000 words take the same time is false in general. The O(1) advantage concerns the number of sequential stages across positions within the layer, not total computation. Standard dense attention compares n positions with n positions, giving n² scores. Its attention computation is O(n²d), and materializing the score matrix takes O(n²) space. Longer sequences require more work; parallelism helps execute it efficiently.

Autoregressive generation also normally produces new tokens sequentially. Parallel processing of known positions does not mean an entire response is generated simultaneously.

Long-range dependencies

A word can directly incorporate information from a distant available position without passing it through every intermediate position. This makes long-range relationships easier to learn than through a long recurrent chain.

The transcript mentions Apple relating to distant terms such as voicemail and music player. These relationships can help interpretation, summarization and response generation.

Limit: Self-attention helps with long-distance context; it does not guarantee that every relevant dependency is learned or that every answer is correct. Context limits and attention masks still apply.

15. Recap of the construction — 34:30–38:56

The speaker revisits the same chain of reasoning:

  1. Apple means different things with phones and juice.
  2. A static lookup vector alone does not identify this occurrence's meaning.
  3. Other words supply context.
  4. Create a weighted combination of word information.
  5. Calculate compatibility scores using dot products.
  6. Use softmax to turn scores into normalized weights.
  7. Introduce learnable W_Q, W_K and W_V to learn useful matching and content representations.
  8. Use the weights to combine values and obtain a contextual output.

Repeated transcript examples are included in their corresponding sections above.

16. Matrix multiplication for the whole sentence — 38:57–42:15

Use one row per token. For [love, Apple, phones], stack three 512-dimensional inputs into X, with shape 3 × 512.

Form:

Q = XW_Q; K = XW_K; V = XW_V

Each projection matrix has shape 512 × 64, so Q, K and V each have shape 3 × 64.

Calculate all raw scores together

S = QKᵀ

(3 × 64) × (64 × 3) = (3 × 3)

Query position ↓ / key position → love Apple phones
love q_love · k_love q_love · k_Apple q_love · k_phones
Apple q_Apple · k_love q_Apple · k_Apple q_Apple · k_phones
phones q_phones · k_love q_phones · k_Apple q_phones · k_phones

Every row describes the scores used to update one position. Every column identifies the position being attended to.

Normalize the scores

A = softmax(S / √dₖ)

A remains 3 × 3. With the row-per-token convention, apply softmax across each row, over the key positions. Each row sums to one.

Correction: The transcript says “vertical direction”. With the QKᵀ layout above, row-wise normalization is required. Its spoken matrix subscripts are also inconsistent; the query-row/key-column table gives an unambiguous convention.

Mix the values

Z = AV

(3 × 3) × (3 × 64) = (3 × 64)

Each output row is the new 64-dimensional representation for one position.

Item Shape in this example
Input X 3 × 512
W_Q, W_K, W_V Each 512 × 64
Q, K, V Each 3 × 64
Kᵀ 64 × 3
Raw scores QKᵀ 3 × 3
Attention weights A 3 × 3
Output Z = AV 3 × 64

For n input positions, the same shapes become n × 512, n × 64, n × n and finally n × 64.

17. The final equation

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ)V

Part Meaning
QKᵀ Calculate query–key compatibility scores
√dₖ Scale using the query/key vector dimension
softmax Normalize over available key positions
Multiply by V Form weighted combinations of value vectors

The transcript derives the unscaled expression softmax(QKᵀ)V and acknowledges the missing division by √dₖ at the end. The supplied text stops before explaining it.

Added clarification: why divide by √dₖ?

Large query/key dimensions can produce dot products of large magnitude. These can make softmax very concentrated and its gradients small. Scaling helps control that effect. If dₖ = 64, divide by √64 = 8 before softmax.

Added clarification: where the 512 and 64 come from

The original Transformer base model used a model width of 512 with eight attention heads, each using 64-dimensional queries, keys and values. The transcript's 3 × 64 output illustrates one head. In the original multi-head block, head outputs are concatenated and projected back to the model width. A transformer also contains other components; a single attention operation is not the entire network.

Added clarification: available context

The transcript's examples allow every word to attend to all three positions. In causal attention used for next-token prediction, future positions are masked. Positional information is also needed to represent order; this transcript does not teach positional encoding, masking or multi-head attention in detail.

18. Assessment answers to remember

What is self-attention?
“Self-attention lets each token combine information from relevant tokens in the same sequence to build a contextual representation.”

Why do we need it?
“The same word can have different meanings in different sentences. Attention helps the model use context, such as phones or juice, to represent Apple appropriately.”

How do you calculate a dot product?
“Multiply matching elements of two vectors and add the results. For [2, 2] and [0, 4], it is 2×0 + 2×4 = 8.”

What are Query, Key and Value?
“Queries and keys determine attention weights. Values provide the information combined using those weights. All three are generated using learned projections.”

Why softmax?
“It converts raw compatibility scores into nonnegative attention weights that sum to one for each query.”

What does the model learn?
“It learns projection matrices such as W_Q, W_K and W_V. Attention weights are then calculated from the current input.”

Why is self-attention useful compared with an RNN?
“It supports parallel computation across known positions and direct connections between distant positions. Standard dense attention becomes expensive for long sequences because it compares every position with every other position.”

One-line memory aid: Project to Q/K/V → compare Q with K → scale → softmax → mix V.
